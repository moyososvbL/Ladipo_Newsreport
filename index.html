    At Ladipo, the cost of keeping cars moving is written into the environment

    A closer look at used oil, waste management and the communities living alongside one of Lagos' busiest automobile trading centres.

    15
    By Group 3: Environmental ReportingHND Mass Communication, YABATECH · Mushin, Lagos State · 6 min read · Meet the team

    In this story

    1. The market
    2. Used oil
    3. Back in the headlines
    4. Work and home
    5. Who is responsible
    6. Beyond Ladipo
    7. Sources

    MUSHIN, Lagos — At Ladipo, the movement of automobile parts is constant. Engines, gearboxes, tyres and other components change hands as traders and mechanics serve customers who come from across Lagos and beyond. But behind the market's commercial energy is an environmental question that cannot be separated from the work taking place there: what happens to the oil, scrap and other waste generated when vehicles are dismantled, repaired and serviced?

    The issue is not simply whether the market looks clean on a particular day. It is whether used engine oil and other automotive waste are collected, stored and taken away safely; whether refuse is kept out of drains and roads; and whether people who live close to workshops and trading spaces are protected from the effects of day-to-day commercial activity.

    Ladipo is an important centre of Nigeria's automobile spare-parts trade. Its scale supports livelihoods and keeps vehicles moving, but the same concentration of trading, repairs and waste-producing activity places pressure on the surrounding urban environment. The challenge is to ensure that the economic value of the market does not come at the expense of public spaces, drainage systems and nearby homes.

    Used oil is not ordinary refuse

    Used engine oil requires careful handling. It should be collected in suitable containers and transferred through appropriate recovery or disposal channels, rather than poured onto bare ground or into drains. The Lagos State Environmental Protection Agency (LASEPA) specifically advises businesses not to discharge used oil into drains or onto land, warning that oil can seep into the ground and pollute underground water. The agency also calls for proper oil-and-grease management and the use of covered waste receptacles.

    What LASEPA advises
    • Do not pour used oil into drains or onto land
    • Manage oil and grease properly
    • Use covered waste receptacles

    The concern is supported by research conducted at Ladipo and another Lagos automobile market. A study published in Environmental Systems Research examined soil samples from spent-oil-affected areas and nearby comparison sites. It reported changes in soil properties at the contaminated locations and found elevated copper in the affected soils, identifying a potential ecological concern if irresponsible disposal continues. The findings underline why visible oil stains should not be dismissed as a minor housekeeping problem.

    Inside the soil study

    Copper stands out in Ladipo's oil-soaked soil

    Researchers compared soil from oil-contaminated spots at Ladipo with clean soil about 800 m away, at two depths (0–15 cm and 15–30 cm).

    • Lead and zinc stayed low. All three metals were below official risk limits.
    • Copper was high and builds up. It binds tightly to soil, so continued dumping adds to it over time.
    • The soil itself changed. pH and phosphorus, a key plant nutrient, both dropped in the oil-soaked soil.

    Who is exposed: mechanics, customers and nearby residents, through soil contact, water from flooding and runoff, and food grown nearby. Lagos' low water table and poor drainage add a risk to groundwater.

    Okebalama et al. (2024), Environmental Systems Research 13:15 · Read the full study summary

    A problem that has returned to the headlines

    Environmental concerns at Ladipo are not new.

    1. 2013

      The market is temporarily closed. Reports describe government concerns about oil and waste pollution, refuse dumped into canals, and trading structures along drainage routes.

    2. 2020

      Traders organise a clean-up and call for a filth-free market, warning that refuse entering a canal is contributing to blocked drainage.

    3. Feb 2026

      The Lagos State Government seals the market over repeated environmental infractions, including refuse dumped on road medians and the highway, and illegal street trading.

    The stated reasons for the 2026 enforcement action should be distinguished from the separate question of used-oil handling; together, however, they show the range of environmental pressures associated with the market and its surroundings.

    Repeated clean-ups and enforcement actions can address immediate problems, but lasting improvement depends on routine waste collection, accessible disposal arrangements, clear responsibilities and consistent compliance by traders, workshop operators and market leadership.

    When commercial space meets residential space

    The environmental story does not end at the market boundary. Around Ladipo, homes and commercial activities can exist close together. At No. 19, Aranotu Street, the field reporting for this project examined a building where automobile-related businesses operate downstairs while people live in the same property.

    That arrangement raises practical questions about the separation of work and home: how are oil, parts and other materials stored; how are noise and movement managed; where does workshop waste go; and what measures are in place to prevent business activity from affecting residents? Interviews recorded with people at the address give the residents' perspective.

    For residents, the quality of the immediate environment is part of everyday life. For businesses, keeping the premises and surrounding area safe is part of operating responsibly. The two interests should not be treated as competing demands.

    Who is responsible for a cleaner market?

    Responsibility is shared.

    Traders and mechanicsSafe storage and hand-over of used oil, clean work areas, no waste in drains.
    Market associationsRegular sanitation, clear disposal rules, working collection arrangements.
    Property owners and operatorsConsider the impact of business activity on people living in the same buildings.
    Public agenciesMonitor compliance, respond to complaints, enforce rules consistently.

    LASEPA lists investigation of land, water and air pollution complaints among the functions of its pollution-control work. The agency's published guidance also sets out expectations for proper used-oil management and waste handling.

    The next step should go beyond periodic closure or a one-day clean-up. It should include reliable collection of used oil, designated storage points, routine removal of general refuse, clear drainage channels, public education and follow-up inspections. Where residents report a problem, their concerns should be documented and addressed, with the relevant operators given an opportunity to respond.

    The question beyond Ladipo

    Ladipo's importance to the automobile trade is clear. The environmental cost of that trade, however, should not be left for neighbouring residents, public drains or the wider city to absorb. A market can remain commercially active while adopting safer waste practices; in fact, a cleaner and better-managed environment is part of the long-term security of the businesses that depend on it.

    The people who work in the market and those who live around it share the same streets, drains and air.

    The test is not whether Ladipo can be cleaned after an enforcement action. It is whether traders, market leaders, property owners and government agencies can sustain standards that protect the environment every day. The people who work in the market and those who live around it share the same streets, drains and air. They should also share the benefits of a cleaner neighbourhood.

    Disposal of spent oil into soils around auto parts markets impacts heavy metal concentrations and poses a potential ecological risk

    Chinyere Blessing Okebalama, Chioma Lucy Onwurah, Chika Mike Jidere and Chukwuebuka Christopher Okolo

    Environmental Systems Research (2024) 13:15 · University of Nigeria, Nsukka and Jimma University

    Read the full paper (PDF) View on the journal site
    In short

    Scientists tested soil at Ladipo and Berger auto markets in Lagos where used engine oil is poured on the ground. The oil changed the soil and made it less fertile. Lead and zinc stayed low, but copper was high and keeps building up, which the researchers call a potential ecological risk.

    Why spent oil matters

    Spent engine oil is the oil drained from cars, generators and machines after use. As an engine wears, the oil picks up metals and dirt such as iron, copper, zinc, lead, cadmium and sulfur. Because of these, the researchers note that dumped motor oil can be more damaging to the environment than crude oil.

    Once it soaks into the ground, it can harm soil health, reach groundwater and enter the food chain through crops.

    How the study was done

    1. Two marketsLadipo Auto Spare Parts Market, around Akinwunmi Lane off Ladipo Street, Papa Ajao, Mushin, and Berger Auto Market on the Mile 2–Apapa Expressway.
    2. Oil-soaked soil vs clean soilAt each market, soil from repair and trading areas was compared with clean soil nearby. At Ladipo the clean site was a weedy area near a canal, about 800 m away. The oil-soaked soil was black, with bits of spare parts and broken glass.
    3. Two depths, three plotsSamples were taken at 0–15 cm and 15–30 cm from three plots at each site: 24 samples in all.
    4. Lab testsAt the University of Nigeria, Nsukka, the soil was tested for texture, density, pH, nutrients and the heavy metals copper, lead and zinc.

    What they found at Ladipo

    Phosphorus53.78 → 14.15mg/kg, clean vs oil-soaked soil
    Soil pH9.05 → 8.40clean vs oil-soaked soil
    Copper6.62mg/kg on average, vs 4.25 at Berger

    Phosphorus, a key plant nutrient, fell by about three-quarters in the oil-soaked soil. The soil's pH and nitrogen also dropped, and calcium was lower than in the clean soil. Organic carbon rose sharply because of the oil itself. Together, the researchers say these changes reduce the fertility and health of the soil.

    Heavy metals in oil-soaked soil (mg/kg)
    MarketDepthLeadCopperZinc
    Ladipo0–15 cm0.116.550.23
    Ladipo15–30 cm0.066.700.21
    Berger0–15 cm0.053.880.32
    Berger15–30 cm0.034.630.34

    All three metals were within the safe range. Copper is the concern because it binds tightly to soil and does not wash away easily, so continued dumping lets it build up toward harmful levels.

    Who could be affected

    The researchers say mechanics, customers, visitors, nearby homes and farms could be exposed to excess copper:

    • Direct contact with soil
    • Water, through leaching, flooding and runoff
    • Food, through crops grown in the soil

    Lagos' low groundwater table and poor surface drainage raise the risk of groundwater contamination.

    The researchers' conclusion

    The disposal of spent oil in the soil poses a potential ecological risk and therefore requires responsible disposal.Okebalama et al. (2024)

    They call for responsible disposal of spent oil to be enforced. They also note a limit of their work: they measured metal levels in the soil but did not assess the actual risk to people's health, which future studies should do.

    Citation Okebalama, C.B., Onwurah, C.L., Jidere, C.M. and Okolo, C.C. (2024). Disposal of spent oil into soils around auto parts markets impacts heavy metal concentrations and poses a potential ecological risk. Environmental Systems Research, 13, 15. doi.org/10.1186/s40068-024-00343-w
